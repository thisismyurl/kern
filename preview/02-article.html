The Last Ferry to Pelee

Every August the Vosloo family closes the cottage the same weekend, and every August someone insists on taking the last boat instead of the first. This year it's Owen, and he isn't alone.

Bram Vosloo September 2, 2026
The Pelee Islander II, dusk crossing, wake catching the last light

The dock at Leamington smelled the way it always did in the last week of August: diesel and whitefish and the particular sweetness of a season ending on schedule. Owen had made this crossing eleven times, always on the nine o'clock, always with his mother's cooler wedged between his knees. This year the cooler was empty and his mother was three weeks gone and the nine o'clock had left without him, which was how he came to be standing on the dock at quarter to seven in the evening, waiting for the last ferry of the day, watching a woman he had never met feed gulls from a paper bag like she owned the wind.

"You'll want to stand on the port side," she said, not looking at him. "The sun does something to the water past the lighthouse. My father used to say it was the only thing on this lake worth being on time for, and he was late for everything else."

Owen said nothing, because he had learned that year that saying nothing was usually the correct response to a stranger who already knew something true about you. She had a way of talking that assumed you'd been listening the whole time, the way his mother used to, mid-thought, as though the conversation had started somewhere in her head an hour before either of you arrived.

The ferry's horn sounded twice, the second call, the one that meant the deckhands were already coiling the last of the rope. He climbed the gangway behind her, and it was only once they were both leaning on the port rail, the harbour lights starting to blink on behind them, that he understood why the crossing felt different this time. It wasn't the boat, and it wasn't the hour. It was that for the first time in eleven summers, nobody on this deck was waiting for him to be someone other than exactly who he was standing there being.

They didn't speak again until the lighthouse. When it came, small, then sudden, then gone, the water did exactly what she said it would: it turned the colour of something you'd want to bottle and couldn't, a gold that had no business surviving a lake this cold. Owen thought of his mother's half-torn-up kitchen, the studs still bare where the cabinets used to hang, and understood for the first time that grief was not the absence of a person but the sudden, specific accuracy of a stranger's prediction about the light.

"She'd have liked this crossing," he said finally, to no one in particular, to the rail, to the gold water, to the woman who hadn't asked.

"They usually do," she said. "The ones who wait for the last boat instead of the first."
